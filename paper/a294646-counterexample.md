# A294646: a counterexample to "a(n) is never 2n-2", and proofs of the other two cases

Chi Hsia, 2026. Written with the help of an LLM (Claude). Sections 1 and 2 are short paper
proofs of classical facts. The counterexample of Section 3 is checked in the Lean 4 kernel;
its minimality rests on computer searches by three independent programs (see Section 3).

A294646(n) = (1/2)^(2n) mod (2n+1), i.e. the least k > 0 with k*2^(2n) == 1 (mod 2n+1).
The entry (Israel and Ordowski, Nov 2017) conjectures: a(n) is never 2, 2n or 2n-2.
Throughout, m = 2n+1 (odd, m >= 3). Since a(n) is the inverse of 2^(2n) = 2^(m-1) modulo m,
and 1 <= a(n) <= 2n = m-1:

* a(n) = 2    <=>  2*2^(m-1) == 1 (mod m)  <=>  m | 2^m - 1;
* a(n) = 2n   <=>  2^(m-1) == -1 (mod m)   <=>  m | 2^(m-1) + 1;
* a(n) = 2n-2 <=>  -3*2^(m-1) == 1 (mod m) <=>  m | 3*2^(m-1) + 1.

## 1. a(n) is never 2 (classical)

Suppose m > 1 and m | 2^m - 1. Let p be the least prime factor of m and d the order of 2
mod p. Then d > 1, d | m and d | p-1. A prime factor of d divides m and is < p, contradicting
the choice of p.

## 2. a(n) is never 2n (classical)

Suppose m > 1 and 2^(m-1) == -1 (mod m). Write m-1 = 2^k*t with t odd (k >= 1). For every
prime p | m, the order d of 2 mod p divides 2(m-1) but not m-1, so the 2-adic valuation of
d is exactly k+1. As d | p-1, p == 1 (mod 2^(k+1)). Hence m, a product of such primes (with multiplicity), is
== 1 (mod 2^(k+1)), i.e. 2^(k+1) | m-1, contradicting the definition of k.

## 3. a(n) = 2n-2 does happen

For n = 9814447404, m = 2n+1 = 19628894809 = 367 * 53484727 and

    3*2^(2n) + 1 == 0 (mod 2n+1),   so   a(9814447404) = 19628894806 = 2n-2.

This is checked in the Lean 4 kernel (theorem `a294646_counterexample` in
`lean/Discovery/A294646Counter.lean`, core Lean only, binary exponentiation proved equal to
`2^e % m`), and by direct big-integer computation (`results/A294646/code/verify.py`).

**It is the least counterexample.** Necessary conditions for m | 3*2^(m-1)+1:
m is not prime (a prime p would give p | 3+1), 3 does not divide m, and for every prime
p | m: -3 is a square mod p (as 3*(2^((m-1)/2))^2 == -1), so p == 1 (mod 6); hence m == 1
(mod 6). Also, by Fermat, p | 3*2^(m/p - 1) + 1, so -1/3 lies in the subgroup generated by
2 mod p, say -1/3 == 2^r, and m/p == 1 + r modulo ord_p(2). Since m == 1 (mod 6), also r == 0 modulo gcd(ord_p(2), 6);
call primes passing all these tests admissible.
A search over all primes p < 2*10^6 satisfying these conditions and all cofactors
k = m/p <= 10^9 in the forced residue classes (`scan.c`) covers every m <= 6.1*10^10
(the least prime factor is <= sqrt(m) < 2*10^6, and the least admissible prime is 61, so
m/p <= 10^9). It found only m = 19628894809. Independently, a plain loop over all odd
m < 1.964*10^10 (`brute.c`) found no other m dividing 3*2^(m-1) + 1 (and none dividing
2^m - 1 or 2^(m-1) + 1), and a separate Go program written by a reviewing agent
(`critic_search.go`: its own admissible-prime list and mod-6 structural search up to
6.1*10^10, a general search without the mod-6 assumption up to 10^10, and exhaustion up to
2*10^8) agrees. As a test of the search logic, the same scan with 3 replaced by 15 finds
m = 987677 and 1589597, matching exhaustion.

The phenomenon is like A050259 (2^k == 3 (mod k)), whose least term > 1 is 4700063497:
for the analogous constant 15, m | 15*2^(m-1)+1 first happens at m = 987677.
